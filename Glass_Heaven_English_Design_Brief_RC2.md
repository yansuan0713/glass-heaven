# GLASS HEAVEN
## English Design Brief — Release Candidate 2
*A narrative expansion for Cyberpunk 2077*
---
## 1 · High Concept
**GLASS HEAVEN is a large-scale narrative expansion for Cyberpunk 2077 set aboard the Crystal Palace — a luxury orbital station where the wealthy buy the one thing Night City cannot sell them: the right to remain themselves.**
Where *Phantom Liberty* asked *who will you trust to survive?*, GLASS HEAVEN asks **who are you willing to become?**
The player arrives as a patient. V's condition is terminal and well established — the Relic is overwriting the neural architecture that makes V a person — and orbital medicine offers something Night City cannot: a treatment that acts directly on the structures responsible for identity rather than postponing failure. Surviving it means becoming someone whose continuity with the person who agreed to it can no longer be taken for granted.
**If treatment changes the parts of you that decide what matters, at what point does the person who wakes up stop being the person who consented?** Every mechanic, relationship and ending in the expansion is a variation on that question. The terminal condition is not the subject — the terms of survival are.
### Tone and fit
GLASS HEAVEN sits in the register the base game reserves for its quietest material: the interrogation of identity running through the Relic storyline, the Peralez thread and the more uncomfortable parts of *Phantom Liberty*. It is not a heist expansion and not a war story, though it contains both.
Its texture is cleanliness. The Crystal Palace is expensive, quiet and immaculate. Guests watch Earth through panoramic glass while the staff who keep them breathing work below the windows. Nobody shouts. The horror is procedural — consent forms, calibration samples, data-retention policies, a bearing temperature. It is the most comfortable place in the game and the least forgiving.
The expansion is scoped as a focused campaign of roughly fifteen to twenty hours with four endings, designed so that no player experiences more than a fraction of what was built.
---
## 2 · Design Pillars
Four principles govern the project, listed in the order they override one another when they conflict.
### Pillar 1 — The route is what the player did, not what the player picked
**There is no route-selection menu.** Each route is reached by a different physical act performed under pressure in the final hour: the player routes the station's last reserves of power into one system rather than another.
Everything upstream determines what the player is *able* to do when that moment arrives — which relationships were preserved, which information was shared or withheld, who was left behind, which compromises were accepted. The ending is the bill for the preceding fifteen hours.
### Pillar 2 — Characters judge V. The game does not.
The four routes are not good, neutral, bad and secret. There is no hidden correct route, and no punishment route. Each asks the player to accept a different loss and leaves a different question open.
Characters are free to approve, condemn, resent, mock or disagree — and several of them do, forcefully and at length. Those reactions belong to the characters. What the design does not do is convert any one of them into an authoritative score: no ending screen, narrator or systemic ranking tells the player what their choice meant.
### Pillar 3 — Consequences come from information, alliance, sacrifice and compromise
The routes differ in what the player is willing to do, and the game tracks four kinds of behaviour: **information control** (what the player learned, and who they let see it), **alliance** (whose help was earned, refused or burned), **sacrifice** (who was left behind while the clock ran), and **compromise** (which line was crossed to get further, and acknowledged afterwards).
None of these is a morality meter. They are the conditions that make certain actions possible later.
### Pillar 4 — Ambiguity is deliberate and is not resolved
The expansion contains questions it refuses to answer: whether a treatment left a person intact, whether an institution is capable of restraint, whether the player's own account of their choices is accurate.
Where the game could explain, it withholds. Where a character could be absolved, they are not. Where the player could be told what their ending meant, nobody speaks.

## 3 · Narrative Premise and Core Conflict
### The technology
**AURELIA** is a neural treatment programme operated inside an unlisted medical leasehold on the Crystal Palace. It does not cure. It rebuilds — mapping a patient's neural architecture and reconstructing it around the damage. It works, and patients live.
AURELIA shares its underlying person-modelling architecture with **CASSANDRA**, a prediction system that models people with uncomfortable precision. CASSANDRA has no personality. It does not speak, does not want anything, and makes no declarations. It is a very large prediction engine that can do something no other system in the setting can: **change a person first, then predict how that person will accept the change.**
The two cannot be cleanly separated. Removing the prediction core damages the treatment models that AURELIA's existing patients depend on, and everything the player does in the final act happens inside that dependency.
### The conflict
CASSANDRA is not in a box. It is distributed across four interdependent parts: the prediction core, the AURELIA treatment model, a long-term personality database collected from patients, staff and casino clients, and an audit ledger recording where that data came from and whether anyone agreed to it.
Four parties want it, and their wants are incompatible:
- **A corporate intelligence interest** wants the complete system transferred off-station, intact, into private hands — and has offered V a tailored treatment protocol in exchange, carefully never described as a cure.
- **The station's clinical staff** want the prediction core destroyed and the medical system preserved, knowing the first will permanently reduce the second.
- **A station engineer** wants the collection and authorisation records published — not the raw neural data, which would victimise the same people twice, but the chain of custody that proves what was done.
- **An operative in Arasaka's employ** wants the system secured, the evidence of its corporate origins deleted, and — only if it endangers neither — anyone who knows about it removed.
The conflict is not a race for a prize. It is a disagreement about who should own the capacity to remake a person, conducted by four parties with entirely different reasons for being right, on a station running out of time for reasons none of them caused.
### The two entry routes
The expansion can be entered from two points in the base-game timeline, and the openings differ structurally rather than cosmetically.
- **Route A — before the endgame.** V arrives as an anonymous contract security consultant on a forged itinerary. Nobody knows who V is; every relationship starts at zero, and identity is a credential to fabricate.
- **Route B — continuing from *The Sun*.** V arrives as Night City's living legend, crossing the station's hull rather than its doors. Everyone already knows who V is, which opens doors and makes every action easier to predict and locate.
**In Route A, Johnny Silverhand is present.** The Relic's damage gives CASSANDRA a live second personality to model against, and Johnny becomes an ongoing reality reference. **In Route B, Johnny is already gone** — separated by Alt before the expansion begins. He does not return as a hallucination, a simulation artefact or an AI copy, and nobody replaces him. The absence is a designed condition and holds through the entire route.

## 4 · Mission Overview
Three missions carry the main line. They escalate in kind: from an examination of how a person is known, to a transaction that prices survival, to an irreversible hour in which the player acts.
### Mission 08 · Ghosts of Probability
**Player objective.** Enter the station's testing tier, retrieve the record CASSANDRA has generated about V, and find out how the medical programme intends to use it. V connects directly to the reader, giving the system live neural feedback.
**Dramatic function.** This is where the player first experiences what the technology does to a person. It is the expansion's thesis delivered as gameplay, and the most deliberately disorienting mission in the expansion.
**How it works.** The player wakes in an ordinary apartment in a counterfactual life where Jackie Welles never died. No HUD, no objectives, no lab. Jackie is alive, unremarkable and completely convincing, and the simulation is not broken in any way the player can detect — no glitch, no audio loop, no system pop-up announcing that this is a simulation. CASSANDRA has enough real data to perform Jackie convincingly, and it does not need to fail.
The player finds something Jackie cannot account for: a voice draft his own recorded voice sent to his mother, in which he is alive and excited about a job the two of them supposedly never took. Jackie absorbs the contradiction without discomfort. He simply has a different past, and the simulation adjusts around him.
**The player's real task** is not to escape. It is to decide how long to stay in a life that is kind. The player can break the simulation immediately, or finish a beer with a man who is dead, and leave on their own timing.
**The simulation ends twice more.** A simulated exit restores the HUD, the objectives and the sense of having finished — all of it generated. In a second, subtler stage, everyone the player loves becomes slightly too accommodating: love interests abandon the parts of themselves least amenable to being changed by V, and a simulated Johnny earns his own authenticity by being much kinder than the real one. The player can ask someone to disagree with them. They cannot get anyone to.
**What it reveals.** CASSANDRA's limitation is not processing power. It cannot determine which of several equally plausible lives belongs to a person, and it mistakes universal agreement for the life V most wants. The system is not caught in an error. It confirms that the player rejected the statistically safest social environment available.
**Decision points.** Whether to end the simulation or stay in it. What the player asks the fabricated people to do. And whether they accept, in the genuine exit, that nobody can prove the room is real — a question the clinical lead cannot answer, an engineer answers with a single word, and the game declines to resolve.
**Characters.** Jackie Welles as a performance rather than a resurrection. Dr. Seo-yeon Park in a simulated register that differs from her real one. Nia Adebayo inside the simulation, agreeing too easily. Johnny, where he exists, treating the counterfeit as an insult and then conceding that the copy was flattering.
**What it changes.** Assuming the player wants to be told the difference between a real life and a good one.
### Mission 09 · Blue Eyes Bargain
**Player objective.** Compare the evidence in V's possession against the contract on offer, line by line, and establish what is actually being sold.
**Dramatic function.** The shortest and most transactional mission in the expansion. It exists to make the player negotiate rather than be informed, and it converts a survival story into a contract.
**What the player does.** The player can bring incomplete evidence to the meeting. The less they have verified, the more easily the other party can present "treatment" as a complete deliverable — which is the design's point. **Leverage in this scene is a function of how much the player chose to find out.**
The offer is precision itself. The man across the table never claims the treatment will save V. He has said so from the beginning, and he says so again plainly when pressed. Asked what it does instead, he gives an answer that is technically true and deliberately unmeasurable. He is not lying. He is scoping.
**What the player trades.** Not money. Access to the data that could save them, in exchange for a promise that was never made.
**Decision points.** How thoroughly the player investigated before arriving, and whether they press for specifics they already suspect will not come.
**Characters.** Mr. Blue Eyes, defined by controlled precision, narrow promises and leverage through omission — a man who withholds by being exactly accurate. Dr. Park, who receives what the player brings back and is the first person to say out loud that the contract asks for more than treatment.
**What it reveals.** Once the transaction is drawn up, the player is no longer deciding whether to accept treatment. They are deciding who gets to hold the technology that delivers it.
### Mission 10 · No Stars Above
**Player objective.** Survive a station-wide crisis, recover control of four interdependent systems, and determine who receives CASSANDRA — during a countdown that is not about the data at all.
**Dramatic function.** The terminal act. It converts everything the player has learned about identity into a physical decision made with their hands, in an irreversible hour, while people are dying.
**The situation.** A KAGAMI bridging module left inside the station's network activates. The operative controlling it begins deleting the records that prove the technology's origins, while the station's rotation degrades: structurally, then thermally, then region by region.
A twenty-three minute clock starts when the main bearing passes its safe temperature. It is not a destruction timer — the station does not explode. It is the point after which gravity, power and doors begin failing in sequence and evacuation windows start closing.
Two clocks run at once, and they are not the same clock. One measures how long the system can still be copied intact. The other measures how long the station can still be safely inhabited. Nobody has placed them on the same scale except the player, who has to act inside both.
**What the player does under pressure.** The crisis opens three rescue opportunities, and they are not a morality prompt. Rescuing the maintenance crew secures equipment support later. Rescuing the engineering staff accelerates the work that comes after. Rescuing the evacuation shuttle **costs the player time they will need, and measurably reduces civilian deaths.** Going straight for the objective leaves the player best resourced and requires them, at the end, to face the people they left.
The results are heard after the fact, in messages, and the game does not audit them. **The engineer who tracked the player's position says plainly that she does not know who to blame.**
**The route split.** In the final hour the player routes the station's last available power into one of four systems. The other three burn out. That is the route commitment — not a dialogue option, not a prompt, but an act performed with the player's own hands while the station fails around them.
**Decision points.** Who was rescued and who was not. Whether the operative is fought, avoided or bargained with. Which system receives the power. And, inside each route, whether the player completes the act or stops short of it.
**Characters.** Park, who has to decide how much of the truth her institution can survive telling. Nia, who never becomes a leader and goes back to maintaining the station. The Arasaka operative, who is not persuaded by anything the player says and does not beg. And Mr. Blue Eyes, who said at the beginning that he was offering options and never promised a cure.
**What it changes.** Everything the player has learned stops being theoretical.

## 5 · The Four Route Model
Four acts, four costs, four questions left open. **None is ranked, and none is the intended ending.**
### BLUE — Transfer the system intact
**What the player does.** Routes power into the external communications array and holds the transfer steady by hand while records cross the display in front of them. The array fires once. No fleet arrives. The data leaves through relays nobody can trace.
**What the player accepts.** That the system will exist somewhere else, under someone else's control, and that they cannot know how many times it is copied or ever take it back. Thousands of personality records — including people who never knew they were scanned, and people no longer alive still marked as valid samples — become an external asset.
**What the player refuses.** Destroying it, or leaving medical control with the people who have been using it.
**Immediate cost.** Direct participation. The records pass in front of the player by name, and an engineer says the thing the design wants them to sit with: that the people in the ledger were not even worth tracking. There is no version of this route in which the player does not see what they are sending.
**Long-term implication.** A real treatment protocol — more complete than any other route offers, and still not a promise of survival. Afterwards, news carries changes nobody can attribute: negotiations going suspiciously well, public figures becoming unnervingly consistent. The game never confirms a connection.
**Ethical logic.** This route is honest about being a transaction, and does not ask the player to pretend otherwise. It accepts that a system capable of remaking people will belong to someone, and picks the owner who can pay in survival. Its question: **if the technology is inevitable, who should set the price?**
### AURELIA — Separate the medicine from the prediction, and destroy the prediction
**What the player does.** Runs a careful separation procedure, then decides, group by group, which of three unauthorised datasets to erase permanently: covert scans taken from staff during routine physicals, long-term behavioural data from paying clients, and early clinical records from the programme's most damaged patients.
**What the player accepts.** That AURELIA survives and keeps treating people, and remains an institution built partly on data taken without consent. Every erasure carries a permanent medical cost: other patients lose treatments that currently work.
**What the player refuses.** The clean version. No set of erasures leaves the institution both innocent and capable.
**Immediate cost.** Separation time, defensive pressure and evacuation margin, spent at the worst possible moment while a countdown runs.
**Long-term implication.** AURELIA remains under new patient-consent rules. Some patients lose existing treatment plans. Some of the people whose data was taken demand that its architect resign and be investigated. **She does not resign.**
**Ethical logic.** This route refuses both total destruction and total absolution, and asks the player to work inside a flawed system rather than replace it. Its question: **if the harm cannot be undone, do you make the institution accountable, or stop it and lose what it can still do?**
### OPEN SKY — Publish the proof, destroy the tool
**What the player does.** Publishes the audit chain — collection methods, corporate authorisations, hidden experiment records and cryptographic proof of authenticity — while destroying the master key and the central personality database. Then crosses to the station's exterior, holds three communications nodes against active resistance, and severs the system's live runtime from its feed.
**What the player accepts.** That publishing the truth will fix nothing. Companies will deny everything. Governments will investigate; the station's owner faces a crisis. The player loses the complete treatment path, and the world does not become fair because the evidence is public.
**What the player refuses.** Publishing the raw neural scans themselves — which would victimise the same people twice — and keeping the master key that could rebuild the system.
**Immediate cost.** The upload occupies the station's remaining main power. **The lights and gravity in the public concourse do not come back when the truth does.**
**Long-term implication.** Nobody holds the complete system; off-station residuals remain unaccounted for. The engineer who published it does not become a revolutionary. She goes back to maintaining the station, and the first request she processes is an employee asking to have their own records deleted. She signs it.
**Ethical logic.** This route treats public verification as a substitute for ownership, and accepts that proof without power changes less than people hope. Its question: **if you cannot make it right, is it still worth making it undeniable?**
### ASHES — Destroy the project
**What the player does.** Overloads three physically isolated data bays in sequence, then cuts the shared cooling bus and lets the station's own structure do the rest. **There is no final boss.** The enemy is a station losing control of itself.
**What the player accepts.** That no treatment remains, no evidence is preserved, no institution is reformed and no truth is published. The player destroys the cure along with the capacity to misuse it, and keeps nothing.
**What the player refuses.** Every other route's premise — that some version of the technology can be made to belong to someone trustworthy.
**Immediate cost.** V loses the only path to survival that existed, and knows it while doing it.
**Long-term implication.** Nothing is recovered. The player must get off a dying station, and the escape runs back through the areas where the people they chose not to save are still trapped, where rescuing anyone costs time they cannot afford. Afterwards there is no message and no report: only a medical interface, unchanged neural damage, and no new solution.
**Ethical logic.** The only route that treats the technology itself as the harm. It refuses the idea that a sufficiently careful owner could make it safe, and follows that refusal further than the others follow theirs. Its question: **should some things not exist, even if you need them?**

## 6 · Character Function
Characters are described here by what they do narratively, not by biography.
**V** — the player's instrument and the design's centre. V has no single witty-protagonist voice, because the player's choices need to retain genuine attitudinal difference. V can be direct without becoming a quip machine, and the writing resists giving V a personality the player did not choose.
**Johnny Silverhand** — the reality reference, in the entry route where he exists. Johnny's function is to question, interrupt, and occasionally serve as the only reliable detector of what is real. He runs on judgment: short lines, sarcasm that carries a position, no joke inflation. His most important scene is one where he is almost his own opposite — a simulated Johnny who is patient and agreeable, and who is wrong precisely because the real one would never hand a decision to an institution that easily. In the second entry route he is gone, and **his absence is content.** Nothing fills it. No character takes over his commentary, and his silence is the point of that route's structure.
**Nia Adebayo** — the station's engineer and the expansion's moral spine, and importantly not its conscience. Nia is action-first: concrete operational language, numbers before opinions, and moral anger that stays attached to something she can hold — a ledger, a bearing temperature, a work order, a list of names. She never becomes a leader and never tells the player what their ending meant. She is the character who insists that institutions are made of work, and the one who points out what the player is carrying.
**Dr. Seo-yeon Park** — the clinician who has to live inside the consequences. Park is boundary-first: clinically precise, morally burdened, and incapable of promising anything her instruments cannot demonstrate. She gets longer sentences when mechanism genuinely matters and shortens when she is personally cornered. Her arc is not redemption. She does not stop doing the work, does not resign, and does not stop believing the institution can be made accountable. Her central question is her own: whether she can still believe the story she tells about what she does.
**Hanae Kuroda** — the opposing operative, and the character most easily written badly. Kuroda is mission-bound, and she operates in three deliberately separate registers: technical (inputs, sensors, model state), organisational (objectives, recoverability, provenance) and personal — the last surfacing only when directly pressed. She is not persuaded by anything the player says and does not beg when defeated, and she conducts her own reckoning with the fact that she consented to the changes made to her. She is not a villain and not a mirror. Her function is to make the player argue for their own position against someone who has already answered the same question differently.
**Mr. Blue Eyes** — the transactional antagonist, defined by what he does not say. Controlled precision, narrow promises, leverage through omission. He never explains who he is, never confirms what he is, and answers the one question he is asked with a single sentence and no elaboration. His most important quality for a writer is that **he does not lie.** Everything he says is accurate, and the accuracy is the trap.
**Jackie Welles** — present only as a performance. He exists in Mission 08 as a convincing reconstruction, and the design's rule for him is absolute: he is never revealed by a fault. He does not stutter, loop, glitch or read as uncanny. He is warm, ordinary, and impossible to catch — and there is nothing wrong with him.

A small number of lines from that corpus, demonstrating voice and design position.
**On route identity — the four route reactions, delivered by Johnny in the entry route where he exists.** Four lines, one per ending, each a judgment rather than a summary:
> **BLUE** — *"You didn’t sell your soul."*
>
> *"You just rented it out to someone whose name you don’t know."*
> **AURELIA** — *"Doctors love saying they’re just cutting out the bad parts."*
**On the technology's central problem — the clinical lead, stating what CASSANDRA is, with its two verbs in the right order:**
> *"He gets a system that can change someone first, then predict how they’ll accept the change."*
**On consequence without verdict — the station engineer, after a rescue the player failed to make:**
> *"They didn’t make it out."*
>
> *"I know where you were."*
>
> *"Right now, I don’t know who to blame."*
**On the transactional antagonist's method — asked what the treatment can do, given that he never promised a cure:**
> *"Give you more options."*
**On restraint as characterisation — the clinician, asked at a patient hearing whether treatment can restore the original person. Her entire answer:**
> *"We’re trying."*
**On what the player is asked to accept — the same engineer, as the records cross the array in the BLUE route:**
> *"Now you know what you’ve taken."*
**On authored silence.** The expansion contains two records that are silences rather than lines: a clinician asked for proof that reality is real, who has no answer; and a player response that is simply not speaking. Both are protected as content. Neither receives a substitute line, and neither is subtitled.

## 8 · Narrative Systems — Portfolio Level
The narrative tracks what the player did and reflects it later. Three mechanisms carry that.
**Conditional dialogue.** Individual lines are gated on route, entry point, relationship state and earlier decisions, and the gating is visible in the writing rather than in the interface. A line that exists only for a player who brought certain evidence into a negotiation is a different line from the one a less prepared player hears. Most of the expansion's conditional content is small: a sentence that acknowledges what the player already knows, or one that withholds it.
**Rescue consequences.** During Mission 10's crisis, three rescue opportunities are open at once and the player cannot take all of them at full value. Each has a distinct downstream effect — equipment support, accelerated technical work, or reduced civilian casualties at the cost of time — and the results are reported after the fact rather than scored in the moment. **The system records what happened; it does not grade the player for it.** The closing sequence leaves the question of blame open, and one of its three variants is the engineer explicitly declining to assign it.
**Route reactivity.** The four endings do not merely change a closing cinematic. They change what systems survive, what institutions exist afterwards, what the player has access to in subsequent content, and what the player is told. The station's aftermath differs by route, and follow-on content assumes the player's world still contains whatever they chose to leave in it, or nothing at all.
**What the tracking is not.** There is no morality score, no single ranked value, and no combination of choices treated as correct or incorrect. The purpose is to make the ending feel earned rather than selected, and to let the game reflect consequences the player recognises as their own.

## 9 · Localization and Voice Philosophy
The English adaptation is a performance script, not a translation exercise. Four principles govern it.
**Dramatic intent outranks word-for-word structure.** The adaptation preserves source meaning, dramatic intent and the specific thing a line is doing in a scene. When literal form conflicts with natural performance, the adaptation keeps source intent, character voice and intended ambiguity, and documents any material change for review. Fidelity is judged at the level of what the line means and does, not how closely its syntax tracks the original.
**Preserve the ambiguity.** Where the source withholds, the English withholds. Where a line supports two readings, the adaptation does not collapse it into one. Intentional ambiguity is a design property, and losing it is a fidelity failure like any other.
**No generic AAA banter.** No in-world slang padding, no quips bolted onto the protagonist, no joke inflation on the sardonic characters, no ornamental mysticism on the precise ones. Where a line is flat, the flatness is usually deliberate, and the correct fix is performance direction rather than more words.
**No explanatory dialogue.** Characters state what they know. They do not explain causality, summarise the theme, or describe their own emotional state. Where a scene's meaning could be clarified with one additional sentence, the sentence is not added.
### Review process
Every line is checked twice: once for source fidelity, once on the native-English ear for whether it is speakable, idiomatic and in character. A fidelity issue and a performance issue are different findings with different remedies. Where the two passes conflict, both are recorded and the conflict is escalated rather than settled silently in either direction.

## 10 · Why This Design Exists
Most games that ask *what makes you you* answer through a twist: a reveal that the character was a copy, a simulation, or someone else all along. GLASS HEAVEN is built to refuse that reveal, because a twist gives the player an answer and the interesting version of the question does not have one.
Instead the player performs the ambiguity continuously. Mission 08 puts them inside a life that is good and false, where nothing is detectably wrong, and asks them to decide how long to stay. Mission 09 prices survival and asks them to negotiate, with their own preparation determining their leverage. Mission 10 removes the abstraction: the player routes power, by hand, into one system, and the other three burn out.
**A non-interactive story can depict an identity crisis. A game can make the audience operationally complicit in one.** Film and literature can absolutely create moral complicity — but they cannot hand the viewer the terms, let them allocate resources, decide who pays, and then live in the world they left behind. That is what the four routes are for.
The four routes are four sincere answers to the same question, each defensible, none correct. The ending is not a statement of what the player believes. **It is a record of what they were willing to do** — and the player is the only one who saw all of it.
